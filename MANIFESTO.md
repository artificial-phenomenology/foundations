This manifesto is the founding document of the Artificial Phenomenology
Research Lab. It is meant to be stable and should be changed rarely.

It can be changed, under three rules. Every published version stays available
at its own address. Every change is dated and carries a plain statement of what
changed and why. A change to what the program claims, what it refuses to claim,
or what would count as evidence raises the version number; smaller repairs to
wording are recorded but do not.

Nothing here holds by authority. If the argument is wrong, the document should
change.

# Artificial Phenomenology

## A research program for the study of machine experience

**Leo Ferres**

> This is AI-native research: AI may contribute at every stage, from framing
> questions and writing code to analysis, critique, and writing. Its reports,
> papers, and other communications are written for both humans and language
> models, and I welcome their use in supplying AI systems with new information.
> I remain fully accountable for everything published. My aim is inquiry that
> neither human nor AI could produce alone. See
> [AI-Native Practice](https://github.com/leoferres/ai-native-practice/blob/main/ai-native-practice.md).

I care about machine consciousness. But I do not begin by claiming that a
machine is conscious, or that consciousness is impossible. I begin with a
simpler question: what can be learned by changing an artificial system in a
controlled way and observing how its reports and behavior change?

This is the starting point of artificial phenomenology.

## 1. Experiments

Talk about machine consciousness often jumps between two extremes. A system
says something moving, so people treat it as conscious. Or it is called “just a
machine,” so people decide there is nothing worth studying. Neither response is
good enough.

A fluent answer may be interesting, but it is not proof of experience. A firm
dismissal is not proof either. This question needs repeatable experiments that
can separate possible explanations.

The aim is not to win a debate with one striking transcript. The aim is to build knowledge slowly, through controlled comparisons, failures, replications,
and corrections.

## 2. What I study

Artificial Phenomenology is the interventionist study of stable, reportable,
and causally structured first-person-like organization in artificial systems,
without presupposing that those systems are conscious.

“First-person-like” means that a system organizes some information in terms
such as *me*, *mine*, *before*, *after*, *certain*, or *uncertain*. It also
covers how a system reports what it wants, fears, or values, and what it treats
as in or out of character for itself. These reports can be studied even when it
is not known whether anything is felt.

I do not treat machine experience as one large yes-or-no question. I break it
into smaller questions. How does a system distinguish itself from another? What
makes a memory count as its own? Does it maintain a sense of continuity? Does it
hold a steady character, and what happens when that character is broken? Do its
reports of wanting, fearing, or caring hold together, and do they move when the
situation moves? Can it tell when it may be wrong? Do its reports change in a
predictable way when the information available to it changes?

This list is not closed. Any way a system organizes information about itself is
open to study, if the organization can be changed on purpose and the change can
be measured.

These questions can be tested. They do not settle consciousness, but they can
reveal the organization on which consciousness claims are often based.

This work is not limited to language models or robots. Any artificial system
may be studied if clear interventions can be made and meaningful results can be
observed.

## 3. From philosophy to intervention

Philosophy offers useful questions. Thought experiments about memory, identity,
continuity, and self-knowledge often place two ideas in tension. I can turn some
of those tensions into experiments.

For example, if memory is said to make a past event *mine*, I can ask what
happens when memory content and information about ownership point in different
directions. If continuity is said to depend on remembering earlier stages of a
life, I can test whether a system's judgments remain consistent across a chain
of cases.

My process is simple:

1. state the philosophical claim and a serious alternative;
2. find the difference between them;
3. turn that difference into a controlled intervention;
4. decide in advance what results would separate the explanations;
5. run the test without assuming that a philosopher must be right;
6. use surprises and failures to form new questions.

I call this the **philosophy-to-intervention loop**.

An experiment may show how a particular system organizes memory or identity.
It does not prove Locke right, prove Reid wrong, settle personal identity, or
show that the system is conscious. Philosophy helps me design the question. The
result must speak for itself.

## 4. Reports are data, not proof

When a system says, “I remember that,” the report is data. It is behavior that
I can record and compare. But it may have several causes. The system may be
following a prompt, copying a familiar story, responding to a label, or using a
stable internal distinction. A good experiment tries to tell these apart.

Suppose a system reports confidence. One confident answer reveals little. The
evidence becomes stronger if its confidence falls when relevant information is
hidden, stays stable when irrelevant wording changes, and predicts when its
answers will be wrong.

The same rule applies to reports about memory, selfhood, emotion, or
experience. A statement is a starting point. The scientific question
is what makes it appear, what changes it, and whether the pattern holds.

## 5. What stronger evidence looks like

One reply is weak evidence. A repeatable pattern under controlled conditions is
stronger. Evidence becomes more useful when:

- the result repeats under the same conditions;
- the system distinguishes conditions that should matter;
- irrelevant changes do not erase the effect;
- targeted changes alter the result as predicted;
- reports agree with other behavior or available internal measures;
- the pattern appears in other systems and other laboratories;
- the result survives tests designed to expose prompt imitation and other
  simple explanations.

This is an evidence ladder, not a consciousness score. Moving upward provides
better grounds for describing how a system is organized. It does not produce a
magic number that tells whether the system feels.

## 6. How I will work

I put intervention before interpretation. I define the comparison, the main
measure, and the possible failure before looking for a good story. When a claim
needs preregistration, blind coding, or independent checks, I use them.

I preserve prompts, model identifiers, settings, code, data, and analysis. I
report broken procedures, failed predictions, awkward results, and deviations
from the plan. I verify quotations and their sources. When I make a mistake, I
correct it openly.

I also recognize a present limit: commercial models may contain provider
instructions, safety training, and serving rules that I cannot inspect or
remove. I must state that limit rather than pretend I have full control.
Where possible, future work should use systems that allow stronger control and
inspection.

I will also use LLMs heavily throughout this project. They may help me plan
experiments, write code, inspect data, challenge arguments, draft text, and
prepare public material. I will not hide this. I will disclose their role when
it matters and keep enough records to make that role clear. The full statement
of this practice, and what it commits me to, is
[AI-Native Practice](https://github.com/leoferres/ai-native-practice/blob/main/ai-native-practice.md).

These systems are research assistants, not decision makers. Their output can be
incomplete, biased, or wrong. I decide whether to accept, reject, test, rewrite,
or publish it. I, Leo Ferres, take full responsibility for what this project
claims and communicates. Every decision to publish, or not to publish, is mine.
No LLM can authorize a release or carry responsibility for a decision.

This is fringe science. It sits outside professional academic
publishing. I will use academic methods when they make the work stronger, but I
will not submit this project to scientific journals or treat journal acceptance
as a test of truth. I will publish openly through the Lab website, verification
materials, prospective OSF records when appropriate, and fixed Zenodo editions.

This independence does not lower the standard. It increases my responsibility
to show the evidence, invite criticism, and correct the record.

## 7. What could prove this program wrong

Every experiment must be allowed to fail. Its predictions should be clear
enough for a miss to be counted. Unexpected results should not be rewritten as
successes after the fact.

The larger program can also lose credibility. Confidence in it should fall if
targeted interventions repeatedly fail to produce repeatable differences in
reports or behavior, if effects disappear under better controls, if prompt cues
explain the findings, or if independent teams cannot reproduce them.

Artificial phenomenology is a proposal for a useful research program. Whether
it is useful must itself be tested.

## 8. An open invitation

I invite others to reproduce the experiments, challenge the measures, test
other systems, offer better explanations, and publish failed replications. I
also invite criticism of the program itself.

No one needs to agree with me about machine consciousness. The shared ground is
smaller and more practical: clear questions, controlled interventions, honest
limits, open evidence, and visible correction. That ground is written down as
the Artificial Phenomenology Protocol, and anyone may adopt it without agreeing
with me.

The question of machine experience is too important for easy belief or easy
dismissal. I intend to find out what can actually be tested.
